# Featured-Research

#### 1) Public Support for State Secession
Survey Research · Public Opinion · Quantitative Analysis

Research examining Americans' attitudes toward state secession using data from the Cooperative Election Study. We find little evidence that partisan motives drive support for state secession. Instead, support is more closely associated with opposition to a strong central government. While most Americans reject proposals to fragment the union, those who oppose secession tend to be motivated by strong national attachments and commitments to the rule of law.

#### 2) The Gendered Governor – Text Analysis of State of the State Addresses & Policy Issues
Text Analysis · NLP · Structural Topic Modeling · R

Using a corpus of more than 3,000 State of the State addresses from 1925–2023, this project examines how governors' gender and political party shape the policy issues they emphasize in their speeches. I use structural topic modeling (STM) to identify recurring policy themes and compare topic prevalence across governors. The findings reveal meaningful gender and partisan differences in gubernatorial agendas, with female governors placing greater emphasis on education, housing, and social welfare, while male governors more frequently emphasize economic growth, taxation, and employment. Republican and Democratic governors likewise differ in the policy areas they emphasize, demonstrating how both gender and partisanship shape political agendas and communication.

Methods: Natural language processing, structural topic modeling, text preprocessing, quantitative analysis, and data visualization.


#### 3) Gendered Candidate Cues & Candidate Evaluation
Survey Research · Experimental Design · Behavioral Science · R

Using two survey experiments, this project examines how masculine and hyper-masculine traits shape voter evaluations of political candidates. Respondents were randomly assigned to candidate descriptions varying in the intensity of masculine cues and then evaluated candidates on vote support and likability. Across both studies, candidates with no explicit masculine cues generally receive the highest levels of support and likability, while more pronounced masculine and hyper-masculine traits tend to reduce evaluations. The findings challenge the assumption that masculinity is inherently politically advantageous and suggest that hyper-masculinity can become a liability. The effects also vary across gender and partisanship, with women generally more critical of hyper-masculine candidates and Republicans showing greater—but not universal—acceptance of masculine traits.

Methods: Survey experiments, randomized vignette design, regression analysis, bootstrapping, interaction effects, and data visualization.
